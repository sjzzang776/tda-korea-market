Persistent Homology for Topological Data Analysis

From a point cloud to a barcode: Čech and Vietoris–Rips complexes, filtrations, persistent homology, persistence diagrams, the structure and stability theorems, persistence landscapes and their $L^p$ norms, and how to turn a time series into point clouds. Statements only, no proofs. Every figure was drawn for this note; the diagrams in the figures were computed with ripser.

Builds on study note 1 (simplicial complexes, chains, $H_n$, Betti numbers, maps induced by inclusions).

0. The problem of scale

A finite set of points has no interesting homology by itself: $b_0$ = number of points, everything else $0$. To see a shape we must connect nearby points. But "nearby" depends on a scale $\epsilon$, and no single $\epsilon$ is right. Persistent homology looks at all scales at once and keeps the features that live for a long range of $\epsilon$.

1. Point clouds and the two standard complexes

Point cloud. A finite set $X=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ in a metric space, usually $\mathbb{R}^d$ with the Euclidean distance $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2$.
Čech complex. Put a closed ball of radius $r$ around every point. $\check C_r(X)$ contains the simplex $[x_{i_0},\dots,x_{i_k}]$ exactly when the $k+1$ balls have a common point: $\bigcap_j B(x_{i_j},r)\ne\emptyset$.
Nerve theorem (statement). The union of the balls $\bigcup_i B(x_i,r)$ is homotopy equivalent to $\check C_r(X)$. So the Čech complex has exactly the homology of the "thickened" point cloud. This is the theoretical justification for building complexes from balls.
Vietoris–Rips complex. $R_\epsilon(X)$ contains $[x_{i_0},\dots,x_{i_k}]$ exactly when all pairwise distances are small: $$[x_{i_0},\dots,x_{i_k}]\in R_\epsilon(X)\iff d(x_{i_j},x_{i_l})\le\epsilon\ \text{ for all } j,l .$$ Equivalently: draw the graph with an edge between any two points at distance $\le\epsilon$, then fill in every clique (every set of mutually connected vertices). $R_\epsilon(X)$ is determined by the distance matrix alone.
Conventions differ between books and software. Some authors write $R_\epsilon$ for "balls of radius $\epsilon$ overlap", i.e. $d\le2\epsilon$; others, and ripser, use the edge length $d\le\epsilon$ (the diameter of the simplex is $\le\epsilon$). Some use $<$ instead of $\le$. The barcode is the same up to a rescaling of the axis, but when you compare numbers across papers, check the convention first. This note and the notebooks use ripser's convention: an edge of length $\le\epsilon$ is present at scale $\epsilon$.
Rips versus Čech (sandwich). With the conventions above, $$\check C_{\epsilon/2}(X)\;\subseteq\;R_\epsilon(X)\;\subseteq\;\check C_{\epsilon}(X).$$ The first inclusion: if balls of radius $\epsilon/2$ meet pairwise, the pairwise distances are $\le\epsilon$. The second: if all pairwise distances are $\le\epsilon$, then any one vertex lies in all the balls of radius $\epsilon$. So Rips can be wrong about a hole at one scale (Figure 3), but never by more than a factor $2$ in scale. Rips is used in practice because it needs only distances and is far cheaper to build.

2. Filtrations

Definition. A filtration of a simplicial complex $K$ is a nested sequence of subcomplexes $$\emptyset=K_0\subseteq K_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K_m=K .$$ For a point cloud, the Rips complexes give one: if $\epsilon\le\epsilon'$ then $R_\epsilon(X)\subseteq R_{\epsilon'}(X)$. Every simplex gets a filtration value, the first $\epsilon$ at which it appears; for Rips this is the longest edge of the simplex.

Each inclusion $K_i\hookrightarrow K_j$ ($i\le j$) is a simplicial map, so by study note 1, Section 8, it induces a linear map on homology $$\iota^{i,j}_*:H_n(K_i)\longrightarrow H_n(K_j),\qquad [z]\mapsto[z].$$ These maps compose: $\iota^{j,k}_*\circ\iota^{i,j}_*=\iota^{i,k}_*$. The whole sequence of vector spaces and maps $$H_n(K_0)\to H_n(K_1)\to\cdots\to H_n(K_m)$$ is called a persistence module.

3. Persistent homology, birth and death

Definition. The $(i,j)$-persistent homology group is the image $$H_n^{i,j}=\operatorname{im}\big(\iota^{i,j}_*:H_n(K_i)\to H_n(K_j)\big)\subseteq H_n(K_j).$$ Its elements are the classes that already existed at step $i$ and are still non-zero at step $j$. Its dimension is the persistent Betti number $\beta_n^{i,j}$.
Birth, death, persistence. A class $\gamma\in H_n(K_i)$ is born at $i$ if it is not in the image of $H_n(K_{i-1})$. It dies at $j>i$ if $\iota^{i,j}_*(\gamma)$ becomes zero or merges with a class born earlier (the elder rule: when two classes merge, the younger one dies). If it never dies, its death is $\infty$. With filtration values $b=\epsilon_i$ and $d=\epsilon_j$, the pair $(b,d)$ is recorded and $d-b$ is the persistence (lifetime) of the class.
Barcode and persistence diagram. The multiset of pairs $\{(b_j,d_j)\}$ for $H_n$ is drawn either as horizontal intervals $[b_j,d_j)$ (the barcode) or as points in the plane above the diagonal (the persistence diagram $D_n$). Points far from the diagonal are long-lived features; points near the diagonal are born and die almost at once. By convention the diagram also contains every point of the diagonal with infinite multiplicity, which makes the distances of Section 6 work.
Reading $H_0$. The $H_0$ barcode of a Rips filtration is the same information as single-linkage clustering: a bar ends at the distance where its component is absorbed by an older one. One bar always lives forever (the whole cloud is connected at large $\epsilon$); the reduced $H_0$ drops it.

4. Why the barcode is well defined: the structure theorem

Theorem (Zomorodian–Carlsson 2005; statement). Let the coefficients be a field. A persistence module $V_0\to V_1\to\cdots\to V_m$ of finite-dimensional vector spaces decomposes, uniquely up to order, as a direct sum of interval modules $$I[b,d):\qquad 0\to\cdots\to0\to\underbrace{F\to F\to\cdots\to F}_{\text{steps }b,\dots,d-1}\to0\to\cdots\to0$$ (identity maps inside the interval). The intervals are exactly the bars of the barcode.

This is the reason TDA software uses field coefficients (study note 1, Section 7): over $\mathbb{Z}$ a persistence module need not split into intervals, and "the barcode" would not be well defined. Over a field, the barcode is a complete description of the persistence module.

How it is computed. Order all simplices by filtration value (ties broken by dimension). Write the full boundary matrix over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ with columns in that order and reduce it from left to right by adding earlier columns (column XOR) until the lowest $1$ of every non-zero column is in a different row. A zero column $j$ means simplex $j$ creates a class (a birth); a non-zero column $j$ with lowest $1$ in row $i$ means simplex $j$ kills the class created by simplex $i$ (the pair $(b,d)=(\epsilon_i,\epsilon_j)$). ripser does this with many shortcuts (cohomology, implicit matrices, apparent pairs), which is why Rips persistence of a few hundred points in dimension 1 takes milliseconds.

5. Reading a diagram: signal, noise, stability

Bottleneck distance. For two diagrams $D,D'$ (each including the diagonal), $$d_B(D,D')=\inf_{\phi}\ \sup_{p\in D}\|p-\phi(p)\|_\infty ,$$ the infimum over all bijections $\phi:D\to D'$ (points may be matched to the diagonal). It is the cost of the best matching, measured by its worst pair.
Stability theorem (Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer 2007; Chazal et al. 2014 for Rips; statement). If two point clouds $X,Y$ are close in the Hausdorff distance, their Rips persistence diagrams are close: $$d_B\big(D_n(X),D_n(Y)\big)\le 2\,d_H(X,Y),$$ where $d_H(X,Y)$ is the largest distance from a point of one cloud to the nearest point of the other. (The factor $2$ is the price of the diameter convention; with the radius convention it is $1$.)

Stability is what makes persistence a usable statistic: small measurement noise moves the diagram only a small amount, so a bar that is much longer than the noise level is a real feature of the data.

Rules of thumb. (1) Long bars = topology of the underlying shape; short bars = sampling noise and local irregularity. (2) There is no universal cut-off; compare bar length with the typical distance between neighbouring points, or with bars obtained from shuffled or simulated data. (3) In dimension $1$, a bar's birth $b$ is roughly the gap between points along the loop and its death $d$ is roughly the loop's size (the scale at which triangles across the loop appear). A loop with few, widely spread points has small $d-b$ even if the loop is real.

6. Summaries of a diagram: persistence landscapes

A diagram is a multiset of points, which is awkward for statistics: you cannot add two diagrams or take a mean. Bubenik (2015) proposed turning a diagram into a function, which lives in a vector space where averages, norms and confidence bands make sense.

Tent functions and landscape. For each point $(b_j,d_j)$ of the diagram define the tent $$f_j(\epsilon)=\max\big(0,\ \min(\epsilon-b_j,\ d_j-\epsilon)\big),$$ a triangle of height $(d_j-b_j)/2$ centred at $(b_j+d_j)/2$. The $k$-th landscape function is $$\lambda_k(\epsilon)=\text{the $k$-th largest value among } f_1(\epsilon),f_2(\epsilon),\dots \qquad (\lambda_k\equiv0 \text{ if fewer than $k$ tents}).$$ The sequence $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots)$ is the persistence landscape. $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge0$ and each $\lambda_k$ is piecewise linear.
$L^p$ norm of a landscape. $$\|\lambda\|_p=\Big(\sum_{k\ge1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\lambda_k(\epsilon)|^p\,d\epsilon\Big)^{1/p},\qquad p=1,2,\dots$$ One number per diagram. For tents that do not overlap, it has a closed form: a single pair $(b,d)$ of lifetime $\ell=d-b$ contributes $\int f=\ell^2/4$ to the $L^1$ sum and $\int f^2=\ell^3/12$ to the $L^2$ sum, so $$\|\lambda\|_1=\tfrac14\sum_j\ell_j^2,\qquad \|\lambda\|_2=\Big(\tfrac1{12}\sum_j\ell_j^3\Big)^{1/2}\quad\text{(non-overlapping tents)}.$$ When tents overlap the sum over $k$ still counts every tent exactly once, so the formula remains exact: the landscape only re-labels which tent is $\lambda_1$ at each $\epsilon$. In practice we evaluate $\lambda_k$ on a grid and integrate numerically.
What the norm measures. $\|\lambda\|_1$ grows with the number of loops and, quadratically, with their lifetimes; $\|\lambda\|_2$ puts even more weight on the longest bars. A cloud with one big persistent loop and a cloud with many tiny loops can have very different norms even if $b_1$ at some single scale is the same. In the finance application the norm is read as "how much loop structure, at how large a scale, does this window of returns have".
Stability of landscapes (Bubenik 2015; statement). $\sup_\epsilon|\lambda_k(\epsilon)-\lambda'_k(\epsilon)|\le d_B(D,D')$ for every $k$, and the $L^p$ norms are continuous functions of the diagram. So a small change in the data gives a small change in $\|\lambda\|_p$.
Other summaries you will meet. Betti curve $\beta_n(\epsilon)$ = number of bars alive at $\epsilon$ (easy, but loses which bars). Persistence image = the diagram smoothed by Gaussians on a grid, weighted by lifetime (a vector for machine learning). Wasserstein distance $W_p$ = like the bottleneck distance but summing $\|p-\phi(p)\|^p$ over all pairs instead of taking the worst one. Total persistence $\sum_j(d_j-b_j)^p$.

8. From a time series to point clouds

Persistence needs a point cloud, but finance gives time series. Two standard constructions:

(a) Sliding window over several series (Gidea & Katz 2018). With $d$ series (here $d$ stock indices) and daily log returns $r_{k,t}=\ln(P_{k,t}/P_{k,t-1})$, each day is a point $x_t=(r_{1,t},\dots,r_{d,t})\in\mathbb{R}^d$. The window of the last $w$ days is the cloud $$X_t=\{x_{t-w+1},\dots,x_t\}\subset\mathbb{R}^d .$$ Sliding $t$ forward one day at a time gives one cloud, one $H_1$ diagram and one landscape norm per trading day.
(b) Delay embedding of a single series (Takens). For one series $y_t$ choose a delay $\tau$ and dimension $m$ and set $x_t=(y_t,y_{t+\tau},\dots,y_{t+(m-1)\tau})\in\mathbb{R}^m$. Periodic behaviour becomes a loop in the cloud; this is the usual way to apply TDA to one signal. Not used in this project, but it is the other half of "TDA for time series".
What a loop in a return cloud means, and a caveat. A persistent $H_1$ class in $X_t$ says that for the last $w$ days the joint returns circled around an empty region instead of filling a blob: the indices moved together in a way that visited several "regimes" without the in-between values. Gidea & Katz read a rising $\|\lambda\|_p$ as the market losing its usual structure before a crash. The caveat: the Rips filtration is not scale-free, so if all returns are multiplied by a constant $c$, every birth and death is multiplied by $c$ and $\|\lambda\|_1$ by $c^2$. A rise in $\|\lambda\|_p$ can therefore come simply from higher volatility. Any report must compare the TDA signal with a plain volatility measure, which is why the notebooks compute realized volatility next to the landscape norms.

9. The early-warning statistics used in the finance notebooks

Given the daily series $y_t=\|\lambda\|_p(t)$, Gidea & Katz follow the "critical slowing down" literature (Scheffer et al. 2009): before a transition a system's fluctuations become larger, slower and more persistent. Over a rolling window of $L$ days they compute

  • the variance $\hat\sigma^2_t$,
  • the lag-1 autocorrelation $\hat\rho_t(1)$,
  • the low-frequency spectral density $\bar S_t$, the average of the periodogram over the lowest frequencies,

and then test whether each statistic rises steadily during the $250$ trading days before a crash, using the Kendall rank correlation $\tau$ between time and the statistic ($\tau=1$: strictly increasing; $\tau=0$: no trend). To know whether a large $\tau$ is unusual, the same $\tau$ is computed on many random $250$-day windows away from crashes; the share of random windows with a larger $\tau$ is the empirical $p$-value.
